Building the health unit: fuel level and battery readings

A second ESP32 that measures how full a demo tank is and how much charge the battery has, and sends both to the car screen over the hotspot. Every step, in order, for someone who's never wired anything before.

Tick each step as you finish it; your ticks are remembered in this browser. If a step doesn't turn out as described, stop there and send the message or a photo to Claude. The main unit (GPS and compass) has its own guide, and keeps working exactly as before.

0

What you have, and what each part does

Second ESP32 DevKit gets the code

The same kind of board as the main unit, but a separate one. It reads the two sensors, works out fuel % and battery %, and sends them over Wi-Fi once a second.

HC-SR04 ultrasonic no code

The board with two silver "eyes". One sends a click of sound too high to hear; the other hears the echo. The time it takes tells the ESP32 how far away the liquid is.

INA219 current sensor no code

The small board with the green screw terminal and the R100 resistor. Put in the battery's + wire, it measures the battery's voltage and how much current flows out.

Buck converter later

Turns the battery's higher voltage down to 5 V for the ESP32. Only needed once you run on the battery (stage 10). It must be set with a multimeter first.

Demo tank no code

A plastic box or wide bottle of water, at least 10 cm across and 10 cm deep. Never a real fuel tank: see Safety.

4 × 18650 cells later

Leave them in the bag until stage 10. Everything before that runs from the laptop's USB.

You also need

  • A breadboard and jumper wires (a second breadboard, or the free end of the main one).
  • Your laptop with VS Code and PlatformIO, already set up for the main unit.
  • A USB data cable: the one that works with the main unit is perfect.
  • Your phone's hotspot, the same one the main unit uses.
  • For stage 10 only: a multimeter (about ₹300) and a battery holder for the 18650 cells.
1

Words you'll see

Trig
"Trigger": the ESP32 pulses this pin for 10 millionths of a second to make the sensor send its click of sound.
Echo
The sensor holds this pin high for as long as the sound took to go to the liquid and come back.
SDA / SCL
The two wires of I2C, how the INA219 talks, exactly like the compass on the main unit. SDA to SDA, SCL to SCL.
Vin+ / Vin−
The INA219's two big screw terminals (and the two pins of the same name). The battery's current flows in at Vin+ and out at Vin−.
Shunt
The tiny 0.1 Ω resistor marked R100. Current through it makes a very small voltage that the INA219 measures.
Series
Cells joined + to −, one after another. Voltages add up: 4 in series is 4 × 3.7 = 14.8 V.
Parallel
Cells side by side, + to + and − to −. Same voltage, more capacity.
mAh
How much charge a cell holds. 3000 mAh gives 300 mA for 10 hours.
Broadcast
A message sent to every device on the hotspot at once. The car screen listens for it.
2

Prepare the parts

  1. Check both sensor boards have their pins fixed on.

    Your HC-SR04 has 4 pins and the INA219 a row of 6, already soldered. Look under each: every pin should have its own shiny solder cone, none touching its neighbour. Loose pins only resting in the holes cause readings that come and go, like the compass did.

  2. Check which HC-SR04 you have.

    Turn it over. If the back shows Trig/Rx/SCL, Echo/Tx/SDA and R4/IIC R8/UART (yours does), it's the newer version that works on 3.3 V. That's why it can share the 3V3 rail and its Echo pin is safe for the ESP32 without any extra resistors.

    If you ever use an older one marked only HC-SR04 with no such text, it needs 5 V and a resistor divider on Echo. Ask Claude before wiring that one.

  3. Pick the demo tank.

    A plastic box or a wide-mouth bottle, at least 10 cm across, so the sound doesn't bounce off the sides, and as tall as you like. Don't fill it yet.

  4. Clear a table, away from the water.

    Wire everything on a dry, non-metal surface. Keep the USB unplugged until stage 6.

The pins say G5 or IO18 instead?

Some boards print G5, IO5 or GPIO5 for D5. They're the same pin. Look for the numbers 5, 18, 21 and 22.

HC-SR04: 4 pins

VCC power in · Trig start a reading · Echo the answer · GND ground

INA219: 6 pins + 2 screws

VCC power in · GND ground · SCL clock · SDA data · Vin− and Vin+: the battery path, used in stage 10. The green screw terminal is the same Vin+ and Vin−, for thicker wires.

4

Connect the wires

about 15 minutes
USB unplugged the whole time

Never add or move a wire while the ESP32 has power. Remember the GPS short circuit: one wrong wire into a rail can make the ESP32's regulator hot in seconds.

Where to place each board

  1. ESP32 across the middle gap, at one end of the breadboard, with the 3V3 / D22 side facing the free row of holes, as on the main unit. Press down on both ends, not on the metal can.
  2. INA219 further along, each of its 6 pins in a different numbered row.
  3. HC-SR04 on long female-to-male jumpers, not in the breadboard. It has to sit over the tank later, so give it wires long enough to reach: slide the female ends onto its 4 pins.

The 8 wires

"+ rail" and "− rail" mean any hole along those long strips on the same side as the ESP32's 3V3 pin. To connect to a pin, use a hole in the same numbered row and same side as that pin.

#FromToColourWhy
1ESP32 3V3+ railredPuts 3.3 V on the + rail
2ESP32 GND (right side)− railblackPuts ground on the − rail
3INA219 VCC+ railredPowers the INA219
4INA219 GND− railblackINA219 ground
5INA219 SDAESP32 D21blueINA219 data
6INA219 SCLESP32 D22whiteINA219 clock
7HC-SR04 VCC and GND+ rail and − railred blackPowers the sensor (two wires)
8HC-SR04 Trig and EchoESP32 D5 and D18orange purpleTrig starts a reading; Echo brings the answer back

Rows 7 and 8 are two wires each, so 10 wires in all. If your wires come in other colours, keep red for power and black for ground.

Check before power

  1. No wire joins the + rail to the − rail, and nothing goes into the same row as both 3V3 and GND.
  2. VCC and GND aren't swapped on either sensor. Read the printed labels again: this caused the GPS short circuit last time.
  3. SDA goes to D21 and SCL to D22, and Trig to D5, Echo to D18.
  4. Tug each wire gently. None comes out.
  5. Nothing is in the INA219's Vin+, Vin− or screw terminal, and no battery is anywhere near. Power comes from USB only.
5

Open it and fill in your settings

5 minutes
  1. Open the project workspace in VS Code.

    File → Open Workspace from File… and choose narrow-road.code-workspace in the project folder. The Explorer on the left now lists health-unit next to main-unit and display-unit. If you already had it open, it may ask to reload: click Reload.

  2. Fill in health-unit/include/secrets.h.

    Open it and put in the same hotspot name and password as the main unit's secrets.h, inside the quotes:

    #define WIFI_SSID "your-hotspot-name"
#define WIFI_PASSWORD "your-hotspot-password"

    Save with Ctrl + S. The health unit needs no account: it never talks to the server. This file never goes to GitHub.

Nothing to change in config.h

health-unit/include/config.h already has the pins above, and starts the battery at 4 cells in series, 3000 mAh. You'll change those by typing commands in stage 10, not by editing files.

6

Load the code onto the ESP32

2–5 minutes
  1. Plug in the health unit's ESP32 with the USB data cable. Its red power light comes on.

    Unplug the main unit while you do this, so you can't upload to the wrong board. The health unit's ESP32 may get a different COM number, which is fine: PlatformIO finds it.

  2. PlatformIO panel → health-unit → esp32dev → General.

    Click the ant-head icon. Under Project Tasks there's now a health-unit group. Make sure it's that one, not main-unit.

  3. Click Upload and wait for [SUCCESS].

    If it sticks at Connecting....., hold the BOOT button until "Writing" appears. If it says could not open port, close any open Monitor and try again.

  4. Click Monitor, then press EN on the ESP32 to see the messages from the start.

8

Set up the demo tank

10 minutes
  1. Fix the sensor above the tank, eyes pointing straight down.

    Tape or clip it to a strip of cardboard or wood laid across the top. Keep the eyes in the middle, away from the walls, and the circuit board above the rim so splashes can't reach it.

  2. Decide your "full" mark at least 2 cm below the sensor, and mark it on the tank with a pen. The sensor can't measure anything closer than 2 cm.
  3. With the tank empty, type empty and press Enter. 
    [Tank] Saved: empty is 22.6 cm from the sensor
  4. Fill the tank with water to the mark, wait for the ripples to settle, then type full. 
    [Tank] Saved: full is 4.1 cm from the sensor

    If it says Full must be at least 2 cm closer than empty, the sensor didn't see the water properly. Check it points straight down, then do empty and full again.

  5. Optional: tell it how much the tank holds when full, to see litres as well. For a 2-litre bottle, type litres 2.
  6. Test it. Type watch, then pour out about half the water. The fuel % drops to about 50. Press Enter to stop watching. 
    [Watch] fuel: 13.2 cm, 51%, 1.02 L | battery: no battery (0.00 V)
Why it doesn't jump around

The sensor takes a reading every 60 ms, and the ESP32 uses the middle value of the last 7. One stray echo off a ripple can't move it. Below 15%, the screen marks the fuel LOW in red.

All of these are saved inside the ESP32, so they survive unplugging. reset forgets them.

9

See it on the screen

Once a second, the health unit sends two short messages to everything on the hotspot:

{"type":"fuel","ok":true,"calibrated":true,"percent":51.0,"litres":1.02,...}
{"type":"battery","ok":false,"volts":0.00,"amps":0.000,"percent":-1,...}

The car screen listens for them and shows them in its side panel, under FUEL • BATTERY:

FUEL • BATTERY
Fuel 51% • 1.0 L
Battery: not connected
  • The screen isn't bought yet. Until then, the Monitor shows the same numbers.
  • The phone's hotspot must be on: it's the "room" the units talk in. The health unit doesn't need mobile data.
  • Nothing goes to the Render server, so the website doesn't change. Fuel and battery appear only on the car screen.

Add the battery (later)

30 minutes, with a multimeter
Only with a multimeter, a battery holder, and the USB unplugged

Li-ion cells can deliver a lot of current. A short across a cell melts wires and can start a fire. Never solder or tape wires to cells; always use a holder.

Choose how to join the cells

LayoutVoltage (empty → full)TypeCan power the ESP32 through the buck converter?
4 in series (4S), 4-cell series holder12.0 → 16.8 Vcells 4
mah 3000		Yes. Needs a 4S charger to recharge
2 in series (2S), one cell measured6.0 → 8.4 Vcells 2
mah 3000		Yes, until about 6.5 V
1 cell (or all in parallel)3.0 → 4.2 Vcells 1
mah 3000		No: a buck converter only lowers voltage. Keep the ESP32 on USB; the INA219 still measures

mah is the capacity of one series string: two strings side by side would be mah 6000.

The battery wiring

  1. Set the buck converter to 5.0 V before it touches the ESP32.

    Connect only the battery to the buck's IN+ and IN−. Set the multimeter to DC volts (20 V range), red probe on OUT+, black on OUT−. Turn the small brass screw on the blue box slowly until it reads 5.0 (4.9 to 5.1 is fine). Many need 10 or more turns. Then disconnect the battery.

  2. Wire it as in the picture, with the battery out of its holder.

    Battery + → INA219 Vin+ (screw terminal). INA219 Vin− → buck IN+. Battery − → buck IN− and the breadboard's − rail. Leave the buck's OUT wires unconnected for now.

  3. Plug the ESP32 into USB, then put the cells in the holder.

    The Monitor now shows the battery. Set your layout from the table above:

    cells 4
mah 3000
[Status] fuel: 13.2 cm, 51% | battery: 15.84 V (3.96 V/cell), 12 mA, 0.19 W, 76%

    Check the volts with the multimeter across the battery: they should agree within about 0.1 V.

  4. To run on the battery alone, unplug the USB first, then connect buck OUT+ → ESP32 VIN and OUT− → ESP32 GND.

    Never connect the buck's output while the USB is plugged in. To use the Monitor again, disconnect OUT+ first, then plug in USB.

How it works out the charge

It starts from the voltage per cell: an 18650 is 4.2 V full and 3.0 V empty. Then it counts the current flowing out, the way phones do, and slowly corrects itself from the voltage while the load is light. Time left = charge left ÷ current. Below 20%, the screen marks the battery LOW.

11

Every time after the first setup

  1. Turn on the phone's hotspot.
  2. Give the health unit power (USB, or the battery through the buck converter). It starts by itself, remembers the tank and battery settings, and joins the hotspot within a few seconds.
  3. Power the main unit as usual. The two don't depend on each other: either works alone.

Upload again only if you change a file (secrets.h, config.h or the code).

13

Safety

Never put the ultrasonic sensor on or in a real fuel tank

It isn't sealed or spark-proof, and petrol vapour is explosive. Petrol vapour also changes the speed of sound, so the readings would be wrong anyway. This project uses a demo tank of water.

  • Keep water away from the boards. Mount the sensor's board above the rim; wire everything else away from the tank.
  • Li-ion cells: use a holder, never short them, don't use dented or warm cells, and charge them only with a proper Li-ion charger. Cells in series slowly drift apart: check each one with the multimeter now and then. If one is far below the others (more than 0.1 V), charge it on its own.
  • Buck converter: always set to 5.0 V with a multimeter before connecting the ESP32, and never connect its output while the USB is plugged in.
  • INA219 limits: up to 26 V and 3.2 A. Fine for up to 6 cells in series and these small boards; not for a car's starter battery.
