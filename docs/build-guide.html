Narrow Road Navigation · main unit

Building the car unit, from the bag of parts to the live map

Every step, in order, for someone who has never wired anything before. Tick each step as you finish it; your ticks are remembered in this browser. If a step doesn't turn out as described, stop there and send the serial monitor text to Claude.

0

What you have, and what each part does

ESP32 DevKit gets the code

A tiny computer with Wi-Fi built in. It's the only part you load a program onto. It reads the GPS and compass and sends everything to the Render server.

NEO-6M GPS no code

Listens to satellites and works out where it is. It sends its position to the ESP32 over two wires, once a second, by itself.

GY-271 compass no code

Measures the earth's magnetic field, which tells the ESP32 which way the car faces. The ESP32 sets it up each time it starts.

Breadboard no code

A board full of holes that joins wires together without soldering. Push a wire in, pull it out to change it.

Jumper wires no code

Short wires with pins on the ends. Male ends have a pin that goes into a hole. Female ends have a socket that slides over a pin.

4 × Li-ion cells not yet

Leave these in the box for now. You need a charger board before they're safe to use. See Safety.

You also need

  • Your laptop (Windows), to load the code and later run the camera program.
  • A USB data cable that fits the ESP32: micro-USB on most DevKit boards, USB-C on some. Many cables that come with phones only charge and won't work. If you have a few, try them all.
  • Your phone with mobile data, for the hotspot.
  • Your Narrow Road account username and password (the one from the Android app).
  • Optional: a USB power bank, to take the unit outdoors or into the car without the laptop.
1

Words you'll see

Pin
One of the little metal legs on a board. Each has a name printed next to it.
VCC / 3V3
Power in. 3V3 means 3.3 volts. VCC on a module is where its power goes in.
VIN
The ESP32's 5-volt pin. It comes straight from USB. You don't use it in this build.
GND
Ground: the "minus" side of power. Every part must share the same ground, or nothing works.
TX / RX
Transmit (talk) and receive (listen). One part's TX always goes to the other part's RX.
SDA / SCL
The two wires of I2C, how the compass talks. SDA carries the data and SCL keeps time. SDA goes to SDA, SCL to SCL.
GPIO 16
A numbered ESP32 pin. It's printed on the board as D16 or RX2.
Firmware
The program that runs on the ESP32: the code in the main-unit folder.
Upload
Copying the firmware from the laptop into the ESP32 over the USB cable.
Serial monitor
A window in VS Code where you read what the ESP32 prints, and type commands to it.
Fix
The GPS has heard enough satellites to know where it is.
2

Prepare the parts

  1. Check the pin strips are soldered on.

    Look at the GPS and the compass. If each has a row of metal pins fixed to it, go on. If the pins came loose in a little bag, they must be soldered on first. Wires just pushed through the holes touch on and off, and the board keeps disappearing. Any electronics or mobile-repair shop can do it in a few minutes. Ask for the pins on the underside, so the board can sit in the breadboard.

  2. Fit the GPS antenna.

    The square, beige ceramic block on a thin cable is the antenna. Press its tiny round plug straight down onto the small gold socket on the GPS board until it clicks. It's delicate: press, don't twist. The beige side must face the sky when in use.

  3. Clear a table.

    Work on wood, plastic or cardboard, not a metal surface: metal under the boards can short their pins. Keep the USB cable unplugged until stage 6 tells you to plug it in.

3

How a breadboard works

Inside, metal strips join some holes together. Knowing which ones is the whole trick:

  • Put two wires in the same group and they're connected.
  • The middle gap separates the two sides. Boards like the ESP32 sit across it, so their two rows of pins don't touch each other.
  • Some long breadboards have the rails split in the middle. If the red or blue line along the edge has a break halfway, keep everything on one half.
  • A module's pin strip goes along the board, each pin in a different numbered row. If two pins share a row, they're shorted together.

Every pin has its name printed beside it. If you can't see the names on top, turn the board over. Always go by the printed name, not by counting positions.

5

Connect the wires

about 20 minutes
USB unplugged the whole time

Never add or move a wire while the ESP32 has power. It only takes a moment for a stray wire to short 3V3 to GND.

Where to place each board

  1. ESP32 across the middle gap, at one end of the breadboard.

    Turn it so the side with 3V3 and D22 faces the edge of the breadboard that has a free row of holes beside the pins. The ESP32 is wide, so the other side has no free holes, which is fine because you don't use that side. Line the pins up with the holes, then press down evenly on both ends until the black plastic strips touch the breadboard. Don't press on the metal can.

  2. GPS a little further along the breadboard, its 4 pins each in a different numbered row. Put the antenna block flat, beige side up.

  3. Compass at the far end, at least 10 cm (a hand's width) from the GPS and its antenna. Its 5 pins each go in a different numbered row. It must lie flat. The X arrow printed on it points to the front of the car later.

The 10 wires

"+ rail" and "− rail" mean any hole along those long strips on the same side as the ESP32's 3V3 pin. To connect to a pin, put the wire in a hole of the same numbered row and same side as that pin.

#FromToColourWhy
1ESP32 3V3+ railredPuts 3.3 V power on the + rail
2ESP32 GND (right side)− railblackPuts ground on the − rail
3GPS VCC+ railredPowers the GPS
4GPS GND− railblackGPS ground
5Compass VCC+ railredPowers the compass
6Compass GND− railblackCompass ground
7GPS TXESP32 RX2 (D16)yellowGPS talks → ESP32 listens
8GPS RXESP32 TX2 (D17)greenESP32 talks → GPS listens

The colours are only suggestions. If your wires come in other colours, keep red for power and black for ground, and write down which colour went where.

Why the GPS is on 3V3, not 5 V

The NEO-6M board accepts 3.3 V to 5 V, and its chip runs on 3.3 V anyway. Using 3V3 keeps every wire on the one side of the ESP32 that has free holes. If you ever do use VIN (5 V) for the GPS, that works too. Never put 5 V on the compass.

Check before power

  1. No wire joins the + rail to the − rail, and 3V3 doesn't share a row with GND.
  2. TX goes to RX and RX goes to TX (wires 7 and 8 cross over).
  3. Tug each wire gently. None comes out, and each sits in a hole of the right row.
  4. No battery is connected anywhere. Power comes from USB only.
6

Install the software on the laptop

30–40 minutes, once
  1. Install VS Code.

    If it's already installed (you've been using it for this project), skip this. Otherwise go to code.visualstudio.com, click Download for Windows, run the installer and accept the defaults.

  2. Install PlatformIO.

    In VS Code, click the Extensions icon on the left (four small squares), or press Ctrl+Shift+X. Search for PlatformIO IDE and click Install. It takes a few minutes. When it asks, click Reload or restart VS Code. An ant-head icon appears on the left: that's PlatformIO.

  3. Plug in the ESP32 now.

    Connect the USB cable from the laptop to the ESP32. A small red LED on the ESP32 should light. The GPS board's LED may light too. If you smell something hot or a part gets warm within a few seconds, unplug at once and recheck stage 5.

  4. Check that Windows sees the board.

    Press Windows+X and click Device Manager. Open Ports (COM & LPT). You should see a line like Silicon Labs CP210x USB to UART Bridge (COM5) or USB-SERIAL CH340 (COM6). The COM number can be anything.

    If there's no "Ports" section, or you see an "Unknown device" with a yellow triangle:

    • Look at the small chip next to the ESP32's USB socket. If it reads CP2102, search "CP210x driver Silicon Labs" and install it. If it reads CH340, search "CH340 driver" and install it.
    • If nothing appears at all, not even an unknown device, the cable is charge-only. Try another.

Open the project and fill in your settings

  1. Open the main-unit folder.

    In VS Code: File → Open Folder…, then choose D:\allen\narrow-road-hardware\narrow-road-hardware\main-unit and click Select Folder. If it asks "Do you trust the authors?", click Yes.

    The first time, PlatformIO downloads the ESP32 tools: 5 to 10 minutes, with progress at the bottom right. Wait until it finishes.

  2. Fill in include/secrets.h.

    In the file list on the left, open include, then secrets.h. Change only the text between the quotes:

    #define WIFI_SSID "your-hotspot-name"         ← your phone's hotspot name
#define WIFI_PASSWORD "your-hotspot-password" ← its password
#define ACCOUNT_USERNAME "your-username"      ← your Narrow Road account
#define ACCOUNT_PASSWORD "your-password"      ← its password
    • Capitals, spaces and symbols must match exactly. Allen Phone and allen phone are different names.
    • Keep both quote marks. Don't add spaces inside them.
    • To find the hotspot name: phone Settings → Hotspot & tethering → Wi-Fi hotspot (the names vary by phone).
    • Save with Ctrl+S. The tab's dot disappears when it's saved.

    This file never goes to GitHub. Don't paste it into chats or screenshots.

  3. Glance at include/config.h, no changes needed.

    It should already have these. If any is different, fix it and save:

    #define SERVER_HOST "narrow-road-navigation-ckml.onrender.com"
#define IMU_COMPASS          ← no // in front of this line
// #define IMU_BNO085        ← these two keep their //
// #define IMU_BNO055

    Under "Ordinary ESP32 DevKit": GPS_RX_PIN 16, GPS_TX_PIN 17, IMU_SDA_PIN 21, IMU_SCL_PIN 22.

  4. Set up the phone hotspot.
    • Turn the hotspot on.
    • If there's an AP band or Band setting, choose 2.4 GHz. The ESP32 can't see 5 GHz Wi-Fi.
    • Turn off "Turn off hotspot automatically" or the timeout, so it stays on.
    • Make sure mobile data is on. The hotspot needs it to reach the internet.
8

Load the code onto the ESP32

2–5 minutes
  1. Open the PlatformIO panel.

    Click the ant-head icon on the left. Under Project Tasks you'll see esp32dev and esp32-s3. Yours is esp32dev (the ordinary ESP32). Click it to open it, then open General.

  2. Click Upload.

    A terminal opens at the bottom. First it builds the program, about 1 to 2 minutes the first time. Then you'll see:

    Connecting....
Writing at 0x00010000... (3 %)
...
Writing at 0x0012c000... (100 %)
Hard resetting via RTS pin...
========== [SUCCESS] ==========

    SUCCESS means the code is in the ESP32. It stays there even when unplugged.

    If it sticks at Connecting.....___.....: press and hold the BOOT button on the ESP32 (next to the USB socket) until "Writing" appears, then let go.

    If it says could not open port or Access is denied: something else is using the port. Close the serial monitor (click the bin icon on its terminal) and any other program talking to the board, then Upload again.

  3. Click Monitor (in the same list, under esp32dev → General).

    The terminal now shows what the ESP32 prints. Press the EN button on the ESP32 (the other small button) to restart it, so you see its messages from the beginning.

When do you need to upload again?

Only when you change a file: secrets.h, config.h or the code. Every other time, just give the ESP32 power and it starts the program by itself.

10

Get the first GPS fix outdoors

5–10 minutes
  1. Take it outside, under open sky.

    Carry the laptop with the ESP32 still plugged in, keeping the breadboard flat so no wires come out. A terrace or open ground is best. GPS doesn't work indoors, and barely by a window. Put the antenna beige side up, facing the sky.

  2. Wait 2 to 5 minutes the very first time.

    Keep the monitor open. The status line changes from GPS: no fix, 0 sats to GPS: fix, 6 sats. On the GPS board, a small LED starts blinking once a second when it has a fix. Later starts are faster (30 seconds or so), because a tiny battery on the GPS board remembers the satellites.

  3. Look for 5 or more satellites. 4 is the minimum for a position. 7 to 10 is a good outdoor result.
11

Calibrate the compass

5 minutes

Magnets and steel near the compass bend its readings, including the GPS, the USB cable and the car itself. Calibrating measures that bend and removes it. The result is saved in the ESP32, so you only do this again if you move the compass.

  1. Stand away from metal. Not at a steel table, not next to a car, and keep your phone and other laptops away from the breadboard.
  2. Type cal and press Enter.

    Over the next 30 seconds, slowly turn the whole breadboard round 2 or 3 full circles, keeping it flat like a plate on a table. Take about 10 seconds per circle. Every 3 seconds it prints how far the readings have swung:

    [Cal] 24 s left | X range 41 uT, Y range 38 uT (each should reach about 60 to 90)
[Cal] Done and saved. Now point the car north and type north

    If it says Not saved: the sensor wasn't turned through full circles, type cal again and turn more slowly, all the way round.

  3. Point the X arrow north, then type north.

    Open a compass app on your phone, then put the phone down a little away from the board. Turn the breadboard until the X arrow on the GY-271 points north. Type north and press Enter. The next status line should say heading 0 (or 359, which is the same).

  4. Turn it a quarter circle to the right (clockwise).

    The X arrow now points east, and the heading should read about 90. If it reads about 270 instead, type reverse, then repeat the north step and check again.

  5. Check all four directions. North ≈ 0, east ≈ 90, south ≈ 180, west ≈ 270. Within 10 to 15 degrees is good for a simple compass.
CommandWhat it does
cal30-second calibration while you turn the board round
north"The X arrow points north right now"
reverseFlips the direction, if turning right makes the heading go down
resetForgets all of it, to start again
helpLists these commands

In the car, faster than about 7 km/h, the unit uses the GPS's direction of travel instead, which is more accurate when moving. The compass covers when you're stopped or slow, such as waiting at a narrow lane. Once the board is fixed in the car, run cal and north again there.

12

See your car on the website

  1. Open the website in the laptop or phone browser: narrow-road-navigation-ckml.onrender.com. Log in with the same account as in secrets.h.
  2. Find the car. On the dashboard (or the admin page's Live map), a car appears at your GPS position. Turn the breadboard and its arrow turns too.
  3. Optional: test an alert. Walk the unit into one of your narrow roads on the map (such as "PARKING"). The dashboard shows the narrow-road alert, just as it did with the phone app.
Your phone app gets logged out

That's expected. One account drives one car device. The ESP32 has taken over the account from the phone, so the phone now only provides the hotspot.

13

Add the phone camera

do this last

Do this only after stages 9 to 12 work. The phone films the road ahead, the laptop measures the road's width, and it sends each reading over the hotspot to the ESP32.

  1. Install the camera program's parts on the laptop (once).

    In VS Code: File → Open Folder, choose the top narrow-road-hardware folder. Open a terminal with Terminal → New Terminal, then type:

    pip install -r camera-unit/python/requirements.txt
  2. Install IP Webcam on the phone (Play Store), open it, scroll to the bottom and tap Start server. It shows an address such as http://192.168.43.1:8080. Write it down.
  3. Join the laptop to the phone hotspot.

    If IP Webcam's address doesn't open in the laptop browser: open a terminal, type ipconfig, and find the Wi-Fi adapter's Default Gateway. That number is the phone. Use http://THAT-NUMBER:8080.

  4. Mount the phone firmly in the car: centred, looking straight ahead and slightly down, so the road 4 to 8 m ahead is in the lower half of the picture. A windscreen phone holder works. If it moves later, calibrate again.
  5. Calibrate the camera once.

    On flat ground, put four markers (stones or chalk crosses) at the corners of a rectangle 2 m wide, from 4 m to 8 m ahead of the camera, centred on the car. Measure along the ground from directly below the phone. Then:

    cd camera-unit/python
python calibrate.py --source http://192.168.43.1:8080/video

    A window shows the picture. Click the markers in this order: near-left, near-right, far-right, far-left. Press Enter. It saves calibration.json.

  6. Run it while driving: 
    python width.py --source http://192.168.43.1:8080/video

    If Windows asks whether Python may use the network, click Allow. The ESP32's status line now ends with camera: 3.2 m, 0s ago. Press q in the picture window to stop.

16

Safety

Leave the Li-ion cells in the box for now

Without a charger and protection board they can overheat or catch fire if shorted or wired wrongly.

  • Never connect a cell straight to 3V3 (a full cell is 4.2 V; that pin takes at most 3.6 V) or to VIN (3.7 V is too little; the board restarts at random).
  • Never join the four cells in series (up to 16.8 V). It can burn out the ESP32.
  • Never let a cell's two ends touch metal at the same time (keys, coins, wires).

Later, the safe one-cell setup: one cell in a proper 18650 holder → a TP4056 charger board with protection (it has pins B+ B− OUT+ OUT−) → an MT3608 boost board, set to exactly 5.0 V with a multimeter before connecting → the ESP32's VIN and GND. Unplug the battery before plugging in USB. The README's "Power" section has the details.

  • Always unplug USB before changing wires.
  • Keep the boards off metal surfaces.
  • In the car, never wire the unit into the car's 12 V system directly. Use a USB car charger.
